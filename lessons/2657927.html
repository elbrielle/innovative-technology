Daily Learning Contract

Topic: Shape constraints in digital composition

Objective: Students will compare how line and circle constraints change visual meaning and apply publication tools to create readable graphics.

TEKS:
  • §126.19(c)(12)(H) — select and use productivity tools found in spread sheet, word processing, and publication applications to create digital artifacts, including reports, graphs, and charts, with increasing complexity.

Demonstration of learning: Two PNG graphics in the worksheet plus a comparison explaining how the different shape constraints changed the result.

Good design speaks without words. Students explore how size, spacing, repetition, direction, and layering change what a viewer notices and feels. Then you demonstrate Canva and students create their two graphics.

Open the teaching deck · Open the existing Canvas assignment

For a 50-minute period, allow about 18 minutes for the review, comparisons and demo, 27 minutes for making and reflecting, and 5 minutes for submission. For a longer period, extend work time while leaving the final slide up. If work carries over, confirm both Canva pages and the worksheet have saved, and resume them next class.

Before class

Open Canva through the school ClassLink account. Have a blank design ready, plus the Lesson 2 assignment and its Google or Word worksheet. Test the worksheet route you will demonstrate. Students need a notecard for the bell ringer.

The assignment stays the same: choose two different words, create one lines-only graphic and one circles-only graphic, export both as PNGs, insert them in the worksheet, and complete both reflections. Submit the Word file or the Google Doc share link through Lesson 2.

Teach the concepts first

SlideWhat you doWhat students notice
1. Pop Art reviewGive two minutes to name two features on a notecard. Take a few brief responses.Recall yesterday’s visual language.
2. Pop Art choicesPoint to dots, bold flat color, thick outlines, comic lettering, and repetition.Each named feature has an example.
3. Good design speaks without wordsName size, spacing, repetition, direction, and layering.These are choices they can make with simple marks.
4. Size creates emphasisAsk what grabs their eye. Click once to reveal the labels.One larger circle becomes a focal point.
5. Spacing and alignmentAsk which feels organized and which feels chaotic. Take a quick A/B vote, then click once.Even gaps and aligned rows feel ordered. Scattering changes the arrangement.
6. Lines can change the feelingAsk which feels relaxed and which feels intense or urgent. Click once after students choose.Gentle curves and open space suggest relaxation. Tight clusters of straighter lines with changing lengths suggest motion and urgency.
7. Repetition and directionTrace the path through each arrangement, then reveal.Repetition creates a pattern. Placement guides the eye.
8. Layering creates depthAsk which circle looks closest and which edge gives that clue, then reveal.Covering part of another shape creates a front/back relationship.

Keep these exchanges short: point, vote, reveal, explain. They are whole-class visual checks, not repeated partner discussions. A student who reads an emotion differently should identify the visual cue behind their interpretation. The labels show the intended reading of these examples.

Slideshow mode: slides 4–8 reveal both labels and their explanation with one click. The next click advances. The cover-and-reveal behavior follows the supplied live-teach deck.

Slide 9: live Canva demonstration

Switch from the deck to Canva. Model the following actions in one short demonstration, about 6–8 minutes. Use your own practice marks rather than completing a student’s final design.

Show thisDemonstrateConnect it to the design idea
A blank workspaceOpen Canva through ClassLink. Start a blank design and name it. Use two pages, one for each graphic.The composition begins with simple marks.
LinesOpen Elements → Shapes and add a plain line. Drag its endpoints to change length and angle. Change its line weight.Length, thickness, and direction change the effect.
CurvesShow an available curved-line choice, or use Tools → Draw for a simple wavy stroke. Select the object afterward before resizing or duplicating it.Straight and curving lines can create different feelings. Freehand strokes and native line elements have different controls.
Repetition and spacingDuplicate a line. Build a short group. Move the copies close together, then farther apart. Show Undo.Students see the mood change while the basic mark stays the same.
Shapes and sizingAdd a native circle from Elements → Shapes. Duplicate it and enlarge one copy.Size creates emphasis. A circle is the only shape needed for the circles-only graphic.
Fill and bordersSelect the native circle. Change its fill. Open Stroke style, select a solid border, and adjust Stroke weight. Show border color where available.The fill is inside. The border defines the edge. These controls depend on the selected object.
AlignmentMove equal circles into a row. Use the alignment guides or Position controls to demonstrate equal spacing.Clear rows and consistent gaps suggest organization.
LayeringOverlap two or three circles. Use Position / Layers to change which sits in front.Layer order changes the visible edge. This is ordinary layering; Combo is not needed for Lesson 2.
ExportChoose Share → Download → PNG. Select the relevant page. Repeat for the other design so you have two image files.PNG saves the design as an image that can go in the worksheet.
Worksheet and submissionOpen the existing worksheet. Insert the lines PNG in its box and the circles PNG in its box. Point to both reflections. Open Lesson 2 and show the file-upload or website-URL submission option your class will use.The completed worksheet is what students submit.

Slide 10: explain the task, then leave it up

At the work launch, point students to the Español tab in the existing Canvas assignment. Students may rehearse their explanation aloud or point to the visual clue before writing. Honor documented response accommodations while retaining the two designs and the explanation of choices. The verified Google worksheet is the “Make a Google copy” link beside the Word-download button, named Graphic_Design_Basic_Shapes_Student_Worksheet.

A comparison might say: “My lines suggest relaxation through curves and open spaces. My circles suggest organization through even rows.” Use this to model pointing to evidence; students explain their own designs in the existing worksheet.

Return to the final work slide. Explain the five choices briefly now that students have seen the design concepts:

  • Rapid: fast.
  • Relaxed: calm or at ease.
  • Complicated: many connected parts.
  • Organized: a clear order.
  • Labyrinth: a maze-like network of passages.

Students choose two different words. One graphic uses only lines; the other uses only circles. They may vary size, spacing, thickness, direction, color, or layering within that constraint. Keep text, photos, stickers, and other shapes out of the two graphics. Words and explanations belong in the worksheet.

Leave slide 10 projected throughout work time. It includes both exemplars, the word bank, completion checks, and the actual Canvas assignment banner. The examples are possibilities, not designs everyone must copy.

During work, check three things in order:

1. The constraint: one lines-only design and one circles-only design.

2. The visual choice: ask, “What did you change to make it feel that way?” Have the student point to a gap, curve, size difference, repeated mark, or overlap.

3. The submission: both PNGs appear in the worksheet and both reflections explain a visible choice. Students submit the completed Word file or the Google Doc link and check the submission preview.

If a student needs support, begin with one line or circle and change one feature. If they finish the main task, the existing extension is a third word using both lines and circles.

For an absent student or a student who needs to replay the demonstration, the existing Lesson 2 Canva video is in the Canvas assignment. It is an alternative to your live demonstration, not another required segment after it.

Standards practice and evidence

Standards status: Practiced. This lesson practices publication-tool use through constrained graphics and written reflection. It does not assess every report, graph, or chart application named in the broader standard.

Evidence: two PNG graphics in the worksheet and reflections comparing how the line/circle constraints and design choices affect the result. Keep the current assignment’s points, rubric, submission settings, and bilingual student resources.
