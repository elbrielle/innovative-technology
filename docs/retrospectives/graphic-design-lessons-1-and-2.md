# Retrospective: Graphic Design Lessons 1 and 2

Recorded September 9, 2026, at the owner's request. The approved second
revisions are the project's teaching exemplars. This retrospective is based on
Elisha's classroom feedback, the initial deck notes, the approved PowerPoints
and speaker notes, their rendered slides, and the release review records. It
does not treat the old Nano Banana slide decoration as the target style.

## What failed

The first pass made the slides cleaner but treated redesign too much as
content summarization. It retained topics while weakening the relationships
among examples, questions, teacher actions, and student work. That produced
decks that looked better than the originals but were harder to teach in the
way the owner intended.

The original slides had two separable qualities: overcrowded visual design
and purposeful classroom moves. We needed to simplify the former while
preserving the latter. The revised decks improved when the teaching sequence
became the design brief.

## Lesson 1: the teacher needed visual evidence to teach from

The initial 14-slide deck had a useful contrast comparison and a strong work
slide. It also repeated a distant soup-can installation image, used a generic
pattern example for repetition, and leaned heavily on discussion prompts.
Several ideas appeared in words or notes without enough visible material for
the teacher to point to.

| Owner feedback | Observed problem | Approved revision | Durable lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| More visual variety; repetition should show Warhol's work | Naming repetition with an unrelated pattern did not explain its role in Pop Art | Slides 4–6 show the soup-can series, a later color variation, and a repeated celebrity image | Choose evidence for the concept and context, not merely an image that fits the title |
| The museum image was too far away | The artwork's useful features could not be read | Close artwork reproductions occupy most of the teaching area | Crop and scale for what students must notice |
| Who used the comic-strip approach? Use comics | “Comics” remained a general style reference | Slides 7–8 show Lichtenstein's work and visible outlines, flat colors, speech bubbles, and printing dots; notes credit Irv Novick as a source illustrator for Whaam! | Name artists and show their work; distinguish adaptation from invention |
| What lettering and colors could students choose? | Advice was less usable than a concrete comparison | Slides 12–15 show the same name in different lettering, actual solid-color pairs, contrast, and dot/outline detail | Place examples directly beside the explanation, then connect them to the task |
| More of the rebellious nature, researched for middle school | The opening provocation lacked enough supporting material | Slides 3, 9, and 10 distinguish a challenge to prestigious fine art, a direct protest print, and demands for museum representation | Preserve the provocative story while grounding each part and avoiding a single sweeping historical claim |
| Opening and final prompts worked; middle discussions were too many | Frequent stems interrupted the teacher's explanation | Keep the museum-object opening and existing name-tag reflection; use the middle for short, evidence-rich explanations | Plan the interaction rhythm rather than adding participation routines mechanically |
| “That is literally perfect” about the work slide | A redesign could accidentally remove the strongest classroom support | Slide 17 preserves the original work slide exactly | Explicit praise plus a preservation request creates a protected artifact, not a suggestion to reinterpret it |

The final instructional deck has 17 slides. More slides here increased useful
examples without increasing discussion stops. The student still makes the
same paper name tag, photographs it, and submits through the existing route.

The historical distinction matters: the revised notes separate Pop Art's
high/low-culture challenge from particular political works and organized
museum protests. They describe unequal representation without claiming women
or artists of color were unable to make art. This retained the owner's
engaging question while correcting a possible overgeneralization.

## Lesson 2: preserve the sequence and the comparison

The first rebuilt Lesson 2 had 17 slides. Slides 3–5 moved quickly to the
assignment and definitions of its word choices, before students had learned
how marks can communicate. The relaxed curves and urgent clustered lines
were split across slides 7–8 under movement-oriented headings. Tool steps
then occupied multiple slides, while size/emphasis appeared later. That
ordering obscured the owner's intended lesson.

| Owner feedback | Observed problem | Approved revision | Durable lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| “We haven't even introduced the concept” | Project meanings arrived before the design examples needed to interpret them | Slides 1–3 recall Pop Art, show its features, and establish “Good design speaks without words”; the word bank is explained at the final task launch | Sequence prerequisites before interpretation and application |
| Review Pop Art, with visible examples | The previous lesson was not functioning as a useful bridge | A notecard recall is followed by dots, solid colors, outlines, lettering, and repetition shown visually | Retrieval should reconnect students to evidence, not become a text-only recap |
| Curves and open spacing suggest relaxation; clusters and lengths suggest urgency | The headings reduced a combined-cue comparison to direction/movement | Slide 6 keeps both drawings side by side and explains the actual cues | Preserve the relationship the source example teaches; do not rename it based on a superficial visual feature |
| “Which one feels relaxed? Which one feels intense?” before words appear | Separate images and visible explanations weakened guessing and reveal | Slides 4–8 use A/B examples with concealed answers, then one-click labels and explanation | Reveal timing is part of the lesson, not decorative animation |
| Teach sizing/emphasis and the other concepts before the app demo | Concepts and software steps were interleaved out of the intended order | Size, spacing/alignment, line feeling, repetition/direction, and layering all precede Canva | Students need an idea worth trying before a list of buttons |
| One live demo with a facilitator checklist, not slides 10–12 of click instructions | The deck duplicated the teacher's demonstration and fragmented attention | Slide 9 is the live-demo handoff; the guide provides the detailed model | End-to-end support means an executable teaching route, not a slide for every click |
| “The tutorial remains available” was empty wording | A support route became an unnecessary projected stop | Replay/absence support stays in the guide and Canvas | Put logistical alternatives where teachers need them, without interrupting the main lesson |
| Preserve a complete work-time screen | Separate task, completion, and submission slides force constant switching | Slide 10 combines the task, two exemplars, word choices, completion checks, submission directions, and actual Canvas banner | Design for what must remain visible while the teacher circulates |

The approved revision has ten slides, with five comparison reveals. Its final
task is still two graphics: one word communicated with lines and a different
word with circles, both PNGs and reflections in the existing worksheet. The
smaller deck is better because it restores the sequence and teacher actions,
not because ten slides is inherently preferable to seventeen.

The guide handles the operational details: a realistic period budget,
save/resume, expected readings, object-dependent Canva controls, export,
worksheet insertion, existing language supports, and submission. Projected
slides keep the examples and immediate student action prominent.

## What to carry forward

The shared success is a coherent classroom sequence with visible evidence.
Preserve useful originals, introduce concepts before application, show
comparisons together, control when answers appear, keep teacher explanations
and student work connected, and leave one complete work slide up. Use concise
copy that a middle school student can act on. Keep real tool screenshots and
historical evidence grounded.

The two lessons also model different solutions. Lesson 1 benefits from a
broader gallery of authentic examples and a few deliberate discussions.
Lesson 2 benefits from consistent A/B layouts and quick reveal checks. Do not
turn either into a universal slide-count rule, a ban on questions, a rule that
all slides must be sparse, or a beige-and-blue theme imposed on every unit.

Preserve the distinction between fidelity and imitation: keep the source's
successful teaching moves and media; rebuild its convoluted decoration when
authorized. Do not erase strong existing material just to demonstrate a new
visual style. Do not treat an approved exemplar as permission to rewrite the
assignment, add assessments, or alter teacher courses.

## Delivery lessons that support the teaching

- New instructional text and diagrams should remain editable. Lesson 1's
  protected final slide is an explicit preservation exception, not a reason
  to flatten every new slide into an image.
- Verify final native delivery. A WebP image disappeared during the earlier
  Pop Art import and was repaired; a good local render did not establish
  Google Slides parity. The later storage release preserved dimensions and
  protected parts while reducing package size.
- Test initial and revealed states in Slideshow mode. Lesson 2's release
  record documents actual Google playback checks for lines and repetition;
  it does not claim PowerPoint desktop animation playback was tested.
- Include descriptive alternatives for meaningful spatial comparisons.
  Lesson 2's final release added seventeen visual descriptions without
  redesigning the approved composition.
- A table that technically fits can still be hard to read. The facilitator
  guide's public version needed readable label-column widths and internal
  scrolling. Inspect the actual guide as well as the deck.
- Preserve the approved visual direction during fixes. Review is meant to
  resolve an evidenced problem, not start another unsolicited redesign.

## How this is now codified

The binding [slide authoring standard](../SLIDE_AUTHORING_STANDARD.md) supplies
the working rules and pre-handoff checks. The [exemplar guide](../exemplars/graphic-design/README.md)
names the exact approved versions, maps teaching moves to slide numbers, and
includes seven stable render previews. Its manifest pins the packages and the
protected work-slide parts. Parent and repository `AGENTS.md` files route
future agents to these materials; the four-lens gate references the new
teaching checks. The separate storage workflow continues to govern file size
and release mechanics.

## Evidence and limits

The owner's direct feedback and approval in this project conversation are the
authority for these preferences. Initial teacher-note companions under
`Graphic Design Teaching Decks/output/review/Teacher notes/` support the
before-state analysis. They are historical drafts, not current exemplars.

The final packages, slide text, notes, and selected before/reveal/work images
were re-inspected for this retrospective. The [Lesson 1 release review](../reviews/pop-art-deck-revision-2026-09-08.md)
documents one agent using four lenses, not four independent agents. The
[Lesson 2 release review](../reviews/lesson2-live-teach-revision-2026-09-09.md)
documents four independent reviewers and a consensus round. The
[storage-maintenance review](../reviews/powerpoint-storage-maintenance-2026-09-09.md)
records the current smaller Lesson 1 package and preservation proof. Do not
upgrade those records into claims of a physical projector test, independent
review where none occurred, or measured student learning gains. The owner
reported the revisions as substantially better; that is design acceptance,
not a controlled outcome study.
