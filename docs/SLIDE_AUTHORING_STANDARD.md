# Teacher-led slide authoring standard

This is the project standard for every new or revised teaching deck and its
facilitator guide. Elisha designated the revised Graphic Design Lessons 1 and 2
as exemplars on September 9, 2026. Follow their teaching decisions, not just their
appearance. Current explicit user instructions take precedence.

Before drafting, read the [retrospective](retrospectives/graphic-design-lessons-1-and-2.md),
inspect the [approved exemplar slides and notes](exemplars/graphic-design/README.md),
and read the current assignment and teacher source. Older initial decks and
generic presentation templates do not override this standard. Earlier guidance
to flatten every slide is superseded: keep new text and teaching diagrams
editable, while preserving explicitly protected source slides exactly.

## 1. Preserve the teaching before changing the design

Inventory what each source slide does: recall, show evidence, invite a choice,
reveal an explanation, model a tool, or support work. Record useful pairings,
animation timing, examples, videos, teacher moves, and protected content before
removing visual clutter. A crowded original can contain an excellent lesson.

Write a short sequence map before producing a deck. For each segment, name:

| Source teaching move | Visible example | Student action | Teacher action | Keep, clarify, or change and why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Two line compositions invite an emotional reading | Open curves beside clustered straight lines | Point or vote, then identify a clue | Reveal and explain the combined cues | Keep the comparison; simplify its decoration |

Preserve the current product, constraints, rubric, submission route, useful
media, and teacher's intended conceptual relationships. Do not invent a new
assessment, assumed prerequisite, discussion routine, or extra product to make
the deck feel complete. Raise a real source contradiction explicitly rather
than quietly choosing a different lesson.

## 2. Give students something to see before asking them to explain it

Build from familiar recall to visible examples, a short explanation, a model,
and application. Teach a new concept before demanding that students use it to
interpret a project word or make a design decision.

Lesson 2's exemplar sequence is Pop Art recall, concrete feature review, the
idea that design communicates, five visual comparisons, one live Canva demo,
then the two-graphic assignment. The project word bank belongs at the task
launch after those examples. This is a sequence model, not a fixed slide count
or a requirement to begin unrelated units with Pop Art.

Each instructional slide has one clear teaching job. An intentional A/B
comparison can contain two examples; the work slide intentionally combines the
information needed for independent work. Neither should be fragmented just to
satisfy a generic one-fact-per-slide rule.

## 3. Make the visual carry the explanation

Use close, readable artwork, diagrams, examples, or real interface captures on
core concept, task, and navigation slides. A visual must show the actual
feature being named. A distant museum photograph is weak evidence for a small
label detail. A generic pattern from a later lesson cannot replace an artist's
use of repetition when teaching that artist.

Show different useful examples across a lesson: artwork, lettering, color
pairings, geometric comparisons, tool context, and student exemplars as the
content requires. Preserve the clean hierarchy and breathing room that the
user liked. Visual richness comes from evidence, not decorative clutter.

Put samples next to their explanation. Show font choices as actual lettering
and contrasting colors as actual pairs. Retain enough context when cropping
that the image's meaning is not distorted. Put creator, work, date, image
source, and factual sources in the appropriate slide notes; retain visible
credits where the exemplar uses them. Never invent historical images or UI.

## 4. Use comparisons with a real reveal

When comparison is the teaching move, keep both examples visible at once.
Ask a concrete question, let students point or vote, reveal the labels and
explanation, and name the visible cues. Do not display the answers before the
guess or separate the pair across slides students must hold in memory.

Describe what the examples actually change. In Lesson 2, spaced gentle curves
and clustered straighter lines differ in curvature, spacing, length, and
arrangement. Together these suggest calm versus movement/urgency. Do not reduce
that comparison to direction alone. The organized/chaotic circle pair changes
alignment as well as gaps; do not claim it isolates spacing only.

Use controlled examples when isolating a single variable. When several cues
change together, explain their combined effect. Emotional labels describe the
intended reading, not a universal law: accept another reading supported by a
visible clue. Use A/B labels and geometry so color is not the only signal.

For a reveal, state the click behavior in the notes and test it in the actual
delivery format. Inspect initial and revealed states. A PDF, image, or package
check does not prove an animation plays. If delivery cannot support the
animation, provide a clearly documented question/reveal sequence rather than
silently losing the teaching move.

## 5. Match interaction to the lesson's pace

Use short teacher explanations and quick whole-class checks where they serve
the example. Do not add a turn-and-talk or sentence stem to every slide.
Lesson 1 preserves its opening museum-object prompt and existing final
reflection; the middle provides evidence for the teacher to explain.
Lesson 2 uses brief point/vote/reveal exchanges. These are different useful
rhythms, not a universal limit of two questions per lesson.

Keep selective, useful language support near the task. Plain student-facing
words such as “Make,” “Ready to turn in?” and “Turn it in” are preferable to
unexplained curriculum terminology. Keep time budgets, standards, expected
responses, misconceptions, source caveats, and alternate routes in teacher
notes or the guide unless students need them to act.

## 6. Make the live demo teachable from the guide

A teacher-led deck can be complete with a concise demo transition slide and a
precise facilitator checklist. Do not replace the requested live demonstration
with a long sequence of isolated click slides. Show the app context and the
few skills students will watch. The guide must specify what to open, what to
select/change, what students should notice, and how the output reaches the
actual worksheet and submission route.

Lesson 2's checklist covers lines, curves, duplication, spacing, circle size,
fill, borders, alignment, front/back layers, Undo, PNG export, insertion into
the worksheet, and Canvas submission. Use the current school account and name
object-dependent controls accurately. Distinguish **Canva** the design tool
from **Canvas** the learning platform.

For compound-shape lessons, verify the **Combo app** and the requested
operations in that account. Ordinary Elements, grouping, and layering are not
substitutes for compound-shape operations. Conversely, do not add Combo to an
ordinary layering lesson that does not need it. Recheck UI before a new release;
the exemplar screenshots document September 2026, not permanent button names.

The existing video can remain an alternative/replay route in the guide and
Canvas. “The tutorial remains available” is not a useful projected teaching
slide. Name the actual route and when to use it in teacher material.

## 7. Leave one complete work slide up

For each independent work block, provide one readable slide that can stay up
for the whole block, including a long 45-minute work period. It must show:

- what to make and its essential constraints;
- one or more relevant exemplars, large enough to use;
- observable completion checks under plain-language headings;
- the exact submitted product, destination, and allowed submission route;
- the real recognizable Canvas module/assignment cue, alongside readable
  module/lesson text; and
- any word bank or reflection students still need while working.

Do not make the teacher cycle among “Your task,” “You are done,” and
“Submission.” A tiny screenshot is a recognition cue, not a replacement for
readable directions. Verify screenshot and text against the current assignment.
Preserve the existing assessment rather than adding a redundant exit ticket.

**Lesson 1's final work slide is protected.** Preserve its slide XML,
relationships, notes, and image bytes, including its existing visual details.
Do not silently rebuild it, renumber its image footer, or “improve” its layout.
Its manifest contains the protected hashes. Only a new explicit user request
can authorize a change to that slide. Other lessons should adapt its function
to their own task, not reuse its name-tag instructions.

## 8. Research accurately without flattening the story

Keep the provocative question that makes a topic matter, and support the
answer with attributable examples. Lesson 1 connects ordinary imagery and
museum prestige to actual protest and representation examples. It does not
equate every Pop artwork with political protest or suggest marginalized
people could not make art. Teach whose work received access and recognition.
Credit source illustrators when discussing adapted comics; do not call a Pop
artist the inventor of comics. Prefer precise, accessible explanation to a
dramatic but inaccurate slogan.

## 9. Review teaching and delivery separately

Before presenting a new revision to the user, answer these questions in the
review record, with slide numbers or other concrete evidence:

1. Which source teaching moves survived, and which changes were intentional?
2. Where can students see each concept before explaining or applying it?
3. Does each comparison preserve the intended relationship and reveal timing?
4. Can a teacher run the demo from the guide without reverse-engineering it?
5. Can students work and submit while one complete work slide remains up?
6. Are actual examples and screenshots readable at projection size?
7. Have unnecessary discussion stops, filler, and planner language been removed?
8. Are task, guide, notes, exemplars, worksheet, and submission route consistent?
9. Are sources, image credits, descriptive alternatives, and non-color cues present?
10. Were the final PowerPoint and native Google delivery checked, with protected
    content preserved and any untested playback stated honestly?

Use the existing [four-lens review gate](CURRICULUM_REVIEW_GATE.md) for changed
instructional deliverables. Automated tests do not certify pedagogy, and an
owner's approval of composition does not excuse a broken import. Keep later
fixes bounded so QA does not erase the approved visual direction.

Follow the [PowerPoint release workflow](POWERPOINT_RELEASE_WORKFLOW.md) for
image size, compression, archiving, and distribution checks. Source maintenance
and authoring rules never expand [fleet write authority](CANVAS_FLEET_PARITY.md).
